\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{LLM agents for process systems and their safeguards}
Generative models are being adopted across process systems engineering, from flowsheet synthesis to operator
support \citep{decardinelson2024genai}. Agentic designs interleave reasoning with actions and revise their
proposals from environment feedback \citep{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion}. In process control, LLM agents have
been used for supervisory fault recovery, with proposals checked against plant knowledge and digital-twin
rollouts before execution \citep{vyas2024agentic,car_todo}. Learning-based control has a long tradition of
guarding a learned policy with a model-based safety layer, such as predictive safety filters
\citep{wabersich2021safety} and shields for reinforcement learning \citep{alshiekh2018shielding}. These
safeguards assume that every action is checked. The present work keeps the safeguard and asks which checks can
be skipped, using information that the agent produces anyway.

\paragraph{Surrogates and multi-fidelity evaluation}
When simulation is expensive, engineering practice replaces part of it with cheaper surrogates of the simulator
\citep{forrester2009surrogate}. An observable screen that predicts the validator's verdict from plant
measurements and the proposed action is such a surrogate: it learns the plant's response. A screen built on the
agent's internals is of a different kind. It does not model the plant at all, but the agent's own propensity to
err. It therefore needs no plant data beyond the validator's labels. Comparing the two kinds of screen, and
their combination, is a central part of this study (RQ1, RQ2).

\paragraph{Selective prediction, deferral and risk control}
Selective classification lets a model abstain when its risk is high, trading coverage for accuracy
\citep{geifman2017selective}; selective question answering applies the idea to language models
\citep{kamath2020selective}. Learning-to-defer methods decide when to pass a case to an expert
\citep{madras2018defer,mozannar2020defer}. Distribution-free methods calibrate such decisions so that a risk
level is guaranteed with high probability \citep{bates2021rcps,angelopoulos2021ltt,angelopoulos2023gentle}. Our
accept rule belongs to this family: an upper confidence bound on the failure rate of accepted proposals is kept
below a tolerance, and the bound is verified once on independent data with an exact binomial interval
\citep{clopper1934}. Two features distinguish our setting. First, the decision has three outcomes, and both
non-deferral outcomes avoid the expensive expert, here the simulator. Second, the screen operates inside a loop
in which a rejection produces a new proposal from a changed prompt. That loop raises a distribution-shift
problem that static selective prediction does not face (Section~\ref{sec:closedloop}).

\paragraph{Model internals as signals of correctness}
Token probabilities carry information about answer correctness \citep{kadavath2022know}, and verbalised
confidence is a weaker substitute \citep{xiong2024verbalized}. Linear probes on hidden states
\citep{alain2016probes,belinkov2022probing} detect falsehoods, latent knowledge and hallucinations
\citep{azaria2023internal,burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,chen2024inside}, and the representations of a model can
encode more about the correctness of its answer than its output reveals \citep{orgad2025llms}. Semantic entropy
detects confabulations from sampled answers \citep{kuhn2023semantic,farquhar2024semantic}, and probes can
approximate it from a single forward pass \citep{kossen2024sep}. These studies ask whether an answer is likely
wrong. We ask a decision question: how many simulator calls such signals let an agent skip at a certified risk,
whether they add to strong observable baselines, and whether they stay safe in closed loop.

\paragraph{Attention as evidence of grounding}
Whether attention weights explain model behaviour is debated \citep{jain2019attention,wiegreffe2019attention};
we use attention only as a predictive signal and make no explanatory claim. Lookback Lens detects contextual
hallucinations from the ratio of attention on the context to attention on generated tokens
\citep{chuang2024lookback}. Our region-based grounding is a targeted variant of this idea. It measures the share
of attention, while each decision-relevant token is written, that falls on the specific prompt region that
decides that token: an adjacency line in path planning, and pre-registered measurements and knowledge-graph
relations in the CSTR. Our results delimit when such a signal is useful. It works when failures are failures to
retrieve the relevant information. It does not work when they are failures of reasoning over information that
was retrieved.
