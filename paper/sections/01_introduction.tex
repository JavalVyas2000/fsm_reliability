\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Large language model (LLM) agents are increasingly proposed as supervisory decision makers for engineered systems.
They suggest recovery setpoints after a process fault, operating sequences for start-up or changeover, or plans
that lower-level controllers then execute
\citep{yao2023react,shinn2023reflexion,decardinelson2024genai,vyas2024agentic,car_todo}. An LLM's proposal can
be wrong in ways that are difficult to anticipate from its wording. Such agents are therefore deployed behind a
\emph{validator}: before a proposal reaches the plant, a simulator or digital twin \citep{tao2019digitaltwin}
rolls the plant forward under the proposed action and checks it against the operating constraints. A rejected
proposal is returned to the agent with the validator's feedback, and the agent tries again. This
propose--validate--retry loop makes the agent safe by construction: no proposal reaches the plant without having
passed a simulation.

The price of this safety is that \emph{every} decision pays for a full simulation. In process engineering the
simulation is often the expensive step. A digital-twin rollout must integrate the plant model over the recovery
horizon; its cost grows with the horizon, the fidelity of the model (lumped versus distributed parameters,
detailed thermodynamics, coupled units) and the number of disturbance scenarios checked, and it can range from
seconds to hours per call. The agent's own cost, in contrast, is fixed for a given prompt: one forward pass per
generated token. As validators become more faithful, they become the bottleneck of the loop, and the question
of \emph{which} proposals actually need simulating becomes an engineering question with direct consequences for
the latency and cost of LLM-based supervision.

This paper starts from an observation about that forward pass. To produce a proposal, the LLM computes, and then
discards, a large amount of information about its own decision: the probability it assigned to each output
token, the attention weights that show which parts of the prompt it consulted while writing each token, and
high-dimensional hidden states at every layer. A growing literature shows that such \emph{model-internal}
signals carry information about whether the model's output is correct
\citep{kadavath2022know,azaria2023internal,burns2023ccs,marks2023geometry,chen2024inside,orgad2025llms,chuang2024lookback}.
If they also carry information about whether a proposal will pass the validator, they offer something a
simulator cannot: a risk estimate that is available at no simulation cost, before the simulator is invoked,
from a signal that does not depend on any model of the plant. The central question of this paper is
therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Can an LLM agent's internal signals replace part of the simulation-based verification of its proposals,
and if so, how much, at what risk, under which conditions, and why?}
\end{quote}

We decompose this question into four research questions, each answered by a pre-registered analysis.
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1 (substitution)] How many validator calls can model-internal signals alone eliminate, with a certified
bound on the failure rate of the proposals executed without validation, when no model of the plant is used?
\item[RQ2 (complementarity)] Do internal signals add to \emph{observable} features of the task state and the
proposed action, which are the natural engineering baseline?
\item[RQ3 (mechanism)] When do internal signals help, and can the reason be measured? We test whether failures
that arise from \emph{not consulting} the relevant prompt information (retrieval failures) are detectable from
attention, whereas failures that arise from \emph{reasoning} incorrectly over correctly consulted information
are not.
\item[RQ4 (deployment)] Do internals-based screens remain safe inside the closed propose--validate--retry loop,
and under which validator costs do they reduce the total compute?
\end{description}

To answer these questions we turn the validator into a three-way router (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). A calibrated
probe reads signals from the agent's forward pass and estimates the probability that the proposal will fail
validation. A confident proposal is executed without simulation (\ACC), a clearly failing proposal is returned
to the agent without simulation (\REJ), and only the uncertain remainder is simulated (\VAL). The accept
threshold is chosen so that a one-sided Clopper--Pearson bound \citep{clopper1934} on the failure rate among
unchecked executions stays below a tolerance, and that bound is then certified once on data that played no
role in building the screen. We compare five families of signals: token confidence, attention mass by prompt
region, hidden states, a targeted \emph{region-based attention grounding} signal that measures whether the model
attended to the specific part of the prompt that decides each answer token, and observable features of the
task and action. Combinations of these families are compared as well.

We study two domains chosen to differ in the type of error the agent makes. In \emph{graph path planning} the
agent must return a path through a directed graph listed in the prompt, and the validator is exact: a step is
legal only if the target appears in the source node's adjacency line. Errors are typically retrieval errors, in
which the model writes a step without consulting the line that decides it. In \emph{fault recovery of a
continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR)} \citep{car_todo} the agent must propose three controller setpoints after
a process fault, and a digital twin validates each proposal by simulation. Here the relevant measurements and
physics are short and salient, and errors are typically reasoning errors in the direction or magnitude of the
correction. Five open-weight models from three families, Qwen2.5 1.5B, 3B and 7B \citep{qwen2025},
Llama-3.2-3B \citep{llama3} and SmolLM2-1.7B \citep{allal2025smollm2}, generate the proposals. All hypotheses,
decision rules and amendments were pre-registered before the corresponding data were examined
\citep{nosek2018preregistration}. Test and certification partitions were evaluated once, and the closed loop
was run on 400 fresh episodes per model.

The contributions of this work are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A formulation of simulator replacement by model internals as certified three-way routing}
(Section~\ref{sec:problem}). We state the guarantee that the bound-based accept rule provides, the
exchangeability assumption under which it holds, and an exact, linear cost model. The cost model yields the
break-even validator cost above which screening saves compute.
\item \textbf{Evidence that the agent's internals alone, with no plant model, remove a substantial share of
verification} (RQ1, Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1}). Internals-only screens skipped 11--79\% of validator calls in
path planning, with certified accepts for four of five models. In the CSTR they skipped 12--53\% of calls,
almost entirely by rejecting proposals, 95--98\% of which indeed failed.
\item \textbf{A fair test of complementarity} (RQ2, Section~\ref{sec:res-rq2}). We compare against observable
baselines of increasing strength: a flexible probe, and features that do not reuse the validator's own logic. In
path planning the internals still add for four of five models under a flexible probe, and add 11--37 points for
all five against validator-free observables. In the CSTR, a flexible observable surrogate of the plant absorbs
their contribution.
\item \textbf{A mechanistic account of when internals help} (RQ3, Section~\ref{sec:res-rq3}). Low attention on
the deciding prompt region predicts failure in path planning (AUROC 0.72--0.88). It predicts weakly or not at
all in the CSTR (0.48--0.72), where a pre-registered test shows that failing proposals attend to the relevant
physics \emph{more}, not less.
\item \textbf{Closed-loop evidence, a failure mode and a deployment rule} (RQ4, Section~\ref{sec:closedloop}). In
2000 closed-loop episodes, trained screens executed far fewer failing actions than random skipping at matched
rates. Probes that use internals accepted failing \emph{retries} for two models. This is a distribution shift
that lies outside the certified guarantee. Restricting unchecked execution to first proposals restored the
guarantee's premise: it reduced failing executions to 0--2 per 400 episodes for the internals-only screens
while still avoiding 29--65\% of validator calls.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work.
